⏺ Both are good directions, and they're related — both are about making the cut
  pieces physically robust. Let me give you my read, because each has one real
  gotcha.

  Experiment 1 — merge corner-touching islands

  The problem is real and precise: two same-layer triangles meeting at a single
  vertex (not a shared edge) are connected by a zero-width point → the cutter
  cuts right through it and the sheet falls apart. My tracer currently emits a
  "bowtie" there.

  - Detection is basically free. In the boundary-edge graph the tracer already
  builds, a pinch vertex is just any vertex with degree > 2 (the boundary passes
  through it more than once). I can list them at zero extra cost.
  - The merge itself needs real polygon union, though. Here's the catch: my grid
  tracer works because triangles are non-overlapping and edge-adjacent (the
  "reverse-edge-absent" test). A hexagon dropped at a vertex overlaps the
  triangles — the tracer can't union that. So to actually
  add-a-hexagon-and-union, I need either a clipping library or fiddly
  hand-rolled loop surgery (which only cleanly handles the 2-arc bowtie case).
  - Your rounding bonus falls out naturally if we go the library route: use a
  small rounded blob (a 12-gon) instead of a hard hexagon at the joint, and you
  get a fillet for free. "Recursing the joins" is then just a second cheap pass.

  Experiment 2 — tabs & slots (glueless)

  Love it, but there's a physical-mechanics catch to resolve first: a tab cut
  coplanar with its island on layer i can't slide into a slit in layer i−1 (a
  parallel plane below it) — not without folding. So "tabs into slits below"
  really means fold-down tabs: the tab extends from the island edge, gets a
  score/fold line at the boundary, folds ~90° down, and pokes through an
  oversized slit in the layer(s) beneath. That's very doable but it's a bigger
  build — it needs score lines as a distinct stroke, slit rectangles subtracted
  from lower layers, the layers thick enough for the tab depth, and assembly
  notes. (The only coplanar version that works is frame-adjacent islands tabbing
  into notches in the frame — but most islands are interior, so that's
  limited.)

  My recommendation: do Exp 1 first (it makes every piece one solid cut, which
  Exp 2 then builds on), pull in a small clipping lib to do it right, and treat
  Exp 2 as a follow-on once we pin the fold mechanism.

⏺ You're right — I was sloppy. A disk union gives you a round notch (the circle
  meets the straight edges at hard concave corners), not a smooth blend. A
  metaball join is what you actually want: the two pieces flare into each other
  with a tangent-continuous concave waist.

  Good news: that doesn't need a clipping library at all — the metaball waist is
  custom geometry either way. The clean way to get it: trace the crisp union,
  find each pinch vertex, and replace the point with a quadratic Bézier neck —
  pull back a short distance L along each edge and curve through the vertex,
  with the control point at the vertex. That's automatically G1-tangent to both
  straight edges (so it flows smoothly, no notch), and L is your
  neck-thickness/smoothness knob. Recursion falls out by re-running the pass.

  But this only works if the tracer treats the two corner-touching pieces as one
  loop crossing at the vertex — my current "pick first unused edge" walk is

⏺ Experiment 1 is built and wired. You were right to reject the disk approach —
  this does true metaball-style necks.

  How it works (src/lib/cut-svg.ts):
  - Pinch detection is free — a corner-touch vertex is any vertex with boundary
  out-degree > 1, straight from the edge graph.
  - Merging uses a new boundary walk: at a pinch, instead of "first available
  edge," it takes the widest reflex turn, which crosses into the touching piece
  and welds them into one loop. (Off = pieces stay separate, unchanged.)
  - The metaball neck: each pinch corner is replaced by a quadratic Bézier with
  its control point exactly at the touch vertex — automatically G1-tangent to
  both edges, so it flares smoothly with no round-notch. Pull-back distance L is
  the neck width. Collinear "pinches" (like a flat shared edge) are skipped, so
  only real notches get a neck.
  - Emitted as Q commands inside the same one-compound-path-per-layer, so it
  stays a single cut.

  In the dialog: a Merge touching islands toggle (default on) + a Join size
  slider feeding L. Verified: bowtie → one welded loop; 4-triangle multi-pinch
  cluster → one strip with necks at every join plus its real center hole;
  typecheck + lint clean.
